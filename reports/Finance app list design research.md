# Keep the feed flat, section the month

**The best-regarded finance apps never section the transaction list by category or by spending type; they keep one reverse-chronological feed and put the fixed/variable/"fun" structure in the monthly overview and in filters.** All ten apps torn down (Copilot, Monarch, YNAB, Rocket Money, Emma, Revolut, Apple Card, Lunch Money, Actual, Spendee) default to a single time-ordered list, and the one explicit "Grouping" control that exists (Monarch web) is not even persisted between visits. RiseUp is the outlier, and it is instructive: its תזרים screen is a sectioned *forecast* (variable, tracked, savings, fixed, income, not-in-cashflow) rather than a transaction browser, and what Israeli users praise about it is the single screen and single green number, while what they hate is its rigidity. The behavioural research points the same way: people spontaneously form about five broad spending groups, controlled trials found no spending benefit from finer category budgets and some evidence that salient categories and precise "left to spend" numbers backfire, and the one robust field experiment (n=3,138) found that a comparison to past spending, not a prettier layout, is what changed behaviour. For finc this means the owner's idea of RiseUp-like sections ("payments, regular, fun, car") is right about the *month* and wrong about the *list*: keep the just-shipped calm one-line row, expose the buckets as a labelled filter with a visible total, and make the Now screen a five-line waterfall (income → fixed incl. installments → savings → variable by group → excluded) with one hero number anchored to a reference point. Installments stay a fixed-section bucket with n/total on the note line, Bit payments to people default to variable spend with repayments netted against the outlay, refunds keep netting into the original category and month, and the RTL number conventions finc already uses (LTR-isolated tabular digits, minus on the left, amount at the trailing edge) are the correct ones; the ₪ position is the only open convention, and no usability evidence settles it.

## Ten leading apps all default to a chronological feed, and sectioning lives elsewhere

The teardown is unambiguous on the first question. **Across all ten apps the default list is a single reverse-chronological feed; none defaults to grouping by category or merchant**, and alternative groupings are delivered as filters, sorts and separate structured views rather than by re-sectioning the list ([Copilot help: Transactions tab](https://help.copilot.money/en/articles/9554412-transactions-tab-overview); [YNAB support: Searching, Sorting, and Filtering](https://support.ynab.com/en_us/searching-transactions-a-guide-r1gxyQryj.md)). YNAB states the rule outright: "By default, transactions in the register are sorted by date in descending order," with larger outflows first within a day, and category grouping exists only in its Reflect "Spending Breakdown", a stack-ranked list of categories that drills into transactions ([YNAB support: Spending Breakdown](https://support.ynab.com/en_us/spending-breakdown-H1H7YxmD0.md)). Monarch's web app has a "Grouping" dropdown with a "No grouping" option, but users complain that grouping and sort order are not remembered, which tells you how peripheral the feature is in practice ([r/MonarchMoney snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/subreddits/MonarchMoney/snapshots/2026-03-13T08%3A55%3A55.164668Z)). Where a "grouping" need is real, the apps answer it with a filter plus a summary: Copilot's filtered results show "total spent, total income, and net" ([Copilot help: Copilot Money for Web](https://help.copilot.money/en/articles/11780342-copilot-money-for-web)), and Monarch rolled out a per-filter summary on mobile in January 2026 ([r/MonarchMoney snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/subreddits/MonarchMoney/snapshots/2026-01-12T17%3A20%3A01.190333Z)). The structured views that do group (Monarch's recurring calendar, Rocket Money's Recurring tab, YNAB's Breakdown, Copilot's Categories tab sorted by spend) are separate screens, not partitions of the feed ([Monarch features](https://www.monarch.com/features/transactions); [Copilot help: Categories tab](https://help.copilot.money/en/articles/9504513-categories-tab-overview)).

Production fintechs that have written up their reasoning keep the feed a timeline. Monzo's unified home feed is "ordered by time," merges items from several sub-feeds, and handles repetition by aggregation rather than sectioning: upcoming payments collapse into one expandable row per direction with a count, summed amount and logos, and pot transfers are deduplicated into one row, with the stated principle that "Clarity matters only where it adds value" ([Monzo blog](https://monzo.com/blog/how-we-unified-our-customers-activity-on-the-new-home-screen)). When a Monzo user asked for grouped feed items, the community pushback was that "a feed should be an accurate timeline" and wrong grouping "is adding a layer of complexity" ([Monzo Community](https://community.monzo.com/t/group-transactions/124588)). Nubank's research found customers organise money into three "moments" (day-to-day in/out and bills; planning; discovering) and used those as top-level tabs, explicitly rejecting organisation by product line ([Nubank Building](https://building.nu.com/how-we-created-tabs/)); the day-to-day moment, note, still contains a single transaction list.

RiseUp is the one product in scope whose central screen is sectioned, and the sections are exactly the ones the owner has in mind. Its help centre walks the תזרים from the bottom up: income, then fixed expenses, then the orange "הפקדות לחיסכון" block, then blue "קטגוריות במעקב", then variable expenses at the top split by week, with a grey "לא בתזרים" bucket underneath for everything that does not affect the calculation ([איך התזרים מחושב?](https://intercom.riseup.co.il/he/articles/8890719); [מה נחשב "לא בתזרים"?](https://intercom.riseup.co.il/he/articles/8995719)). But this is a forecast ledger, not a list: the worked example is arithmetic (15,000 ₪ income − 5,000 fixed − 1,000 savings − 2,500 tracked = 6,500 ₪ variable), the headline is "המספר שבצבע ירוק" showing what is left for variable spending this week, and RiseUp recommends keeping **no more than four tracked categories** ([כמה כסף נשאר להוציא בכל קטגוריה?](https://intercom.riseup.co.il/he/articles/8976343); [מה לבדוק כשהתזרים שלילי?](https://intercom.riseup.co.il/he/articles/6228720)). Israeli users' praise is about that reduction: on HaSolidit one user credits the single main screen and variable/fixed tracking with lifting monthly surplus from ~2,500 ₪ to 6,000–12,000 ₪ ([hasolidit.com thread 31779](https://www.hasolidit.com/kehila/threads/אפליקציה-לניהול-תקציב-משפחתי.31779/)). The complaints are about rigidity that follows from treating sections as the primary object: fixed expenses "cannot be split" because "בחרנו לשמור על פשטות", moving a transaction between months is only possible through the chat bot, and there is no manual or cash entry ([פיצול הוצאות בתזרים](https://intercom.riseup.co.il/he/articles/6608891); [להעביר עסקאות בין חודשים](https://intercom.riseup.co.il/he/articles/11081681); [App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/il/app/riseup-רייזאפ/id6739494831?see-all=reviews&platform=iphone)). The Israeli card apps go the other way again: Max, Cal and Isracard organise the list around the billing cycle and lead with "החיוב הקרוב", with Isracard's February 2025 release reorganising transactions into current-cycle, non-current and future-dated groups ([Isracard App Store](https://apps.apple.com/il/app/isracard-ישראכרט/id489783331); [Cal App Store](https://apps.apple.com/il/app/cal-כאל/id586226158)). Nobody in the field sections a transaction list by spending type.

## Research favours headers and few broad groups, not a category-sectioned ledger

The UX evidence explains why the industry converged this way, and it is subtler than "flat is good." Eye-tracking shows that pages with clear headings are scanned in the efficient "layer-cake" pattern, "by far the most effective way to scan pages," while heading-less pages fall back to the F-pattern and users miss content ([NN/g: Layer-Cake Pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/)). A CHI 2001 experiment on search results found that "all Category interfaces were more effective than List interfaces" ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/optimizing-search-by-showing-results-in-context/)). So some sectioning helps. But the same body of research sets hard limits on depth and density: designs "that go beyond 2 disclosure levels typically have low usability," so list → detail is already the maximum before a group → sub-group → detail hierarchy becomes risky ([NN/g: Progressive Disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)); thirteen usability tests of condensed versus dispersed layouts found users had more difficulty on the low-density page and "showed a clear preference for a condensed layout" ([NN/g: Content Dispersion](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/content-dispersion/)); and sticky headers "permanently use screen space on every page," justified only when users need the header's items while scrolling ([NN/g: Sticky Headers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/sticky-headers/)). **No controlled study compares day-grouped against category-grouped transaction lists**, so the choice between them is inference: date headers serve the "accurate timeline" reconciliation task, category grouping serves review and overview, and the apps have resolved this by giving the timeline to the list and the categories to the overview.

The behavioural literature then says how coarse the overview's groups should be. When 64 expenditures were pile-sorted, "five was the mode of the number of groups that participants constructed (range = 3–18)," the consensus groups were bills, groceries, household products, clothing and entertainment, and the two underlying dimensions were typical spend and necessity ([Fei, Bartels & Zhang, BFI WP 2024-159](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/BFI_WP_2024-159.pdf)). In a survey of 3,800 US consumers, 90% of budgeters tracked spending in distinct categories, but granularity ranged from people who "simply separated bills from discretionary spending" to line-item trackers ([Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/2023/february/five-insights-about-budgeting)). Finer control did not buy outcomes: in a 13-week RCT with **9,035 users**, a one-number weekly budget and a category budget produced statistically identical spending ($681 vs $673 vs $676 control, all p>0.4), budgeters spent 1.3–1.4 times what they intended, and spending in *budgeted* categories ran about $30 higher than in unbudgeted ones ([Common Cents Lab: Clarity Money](https://finsights.guide/case-studies/GfCI1P8I/clarity-money-budgeting-experiment)). A ~400-person lab study found that making budget categories more salient *increased* a tempting purchase ([Common Cents Lab: Budget categories](https://finsights.guide/case-studies/t8a0U930/exploring-how-budget-categories-and-opportunity-co)), and work on dynamic budget monitoring found that showing a precise remaining balance raises spending late in the period and that "breaking a big budget into multiple small budgets, increased total spending" ([Pocheptsova Ghosh & Huang, CEPR](https://cepr.org/system/files/2022-08/Dynamic%20Budget%20Monitoring%20-%20When%20Access%20to%20Budget%20Feedback%20Leads%20to%20Increase%20in%20Spending%20-%20Anastasiya%20Pocheptsova%20Ghosh%20&%20Liang%20Huang.pdf)). Exhausting a narrow budget also lowers satisfaction with the purchase, the "bottom dollar effect" ([Soster, Gershoff & Bearden 2014](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:doi:10.1086/677223)). The one bucket with direct empirical support is the pooled irregular pot: people "spent almost double as much as they expected to on exceptional expenses" because each is construed as unique, and the remedy is to treat them as part of a larger set ([NYU Stern on Sussman & Alter](https://www.stern.nyu.edu/experience-stern/faculty-research/adam-alter-exceptional-expense)).

For finc, which already has 33 leaf categories in ten groups, the implication is that the 33 leaves are for *attribution* and the groups are the *behavioural* unit. That matches where the industry has landed: Monarch's group-level budgeting exists to "set some guardrails around your spending, without getting stuck in the nitty gritty" ([Monarch: Group-level budgeting](https://www.monarch.com/group-level-budgeting)), Copilot's "Unassigned Budget" per group exists because spend "shifts between cooking and dining out" ([Copilot help: Groups](https://intercom.help/copilotmoney/en/articles/3767655-groups-of-categories)), and Lunch Money's Budgeting v2 added a per-group toggle for the same reason ([Lunch Money: Budgeting v2](https://support.lunchmoney.app/migrating-to-budgeting-v2)). The counter-evidence from Monarch's mobile users is worth noting: "I can only see the total for the group, there's no way to see that $400 was dining and $1600 was grocery" ([r/MonarchMoney snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/subreddits/MonarchMoney/snapshots/2026-01-24T07%3A40%3A22.40417Z)), so a group line must open into its categories or its filtered transactions in one tap, which stays inside the two-level limit.

## Fixed, flex and fun: three models, one residual number, no "fun money" anywhere

The second half of the owner's question, how fixed versus variable versus fun should be presented, has three working models in the field. Monarch's default "flex budgeting" renders three buckets as sections of the budget screen with an explicit formula, "Income − Fixed Expenses − Non-monthly expenses − Goals = Monthly Flex Number," where Fixed is "predictable, recurring expenses that don't change much month to month and can't easily be reduced," Non-monthly covers taxes, gifts, repairs, travel and annual premiums, and Flexible is "the expenses that are hardest to predict, but easiest for you to control," of which Monarch says "Some are needs, and some are wants" ([Monarch blog: Fixed/Flex spending](https://www.monarch.com/blog/fixed-flex-spending-what-it-is-and-how-to-get-started)). The second model is a single residual: Copilot's dashboard leads with "Free to Spend," excludes expected recurrings from the pace chart because "Large early-month payments, such as rent or a mortgage, can cause spikes" ([Copilot help: Dashboard](https://intercom.help/copilotmoney/en/articles/6045480-dashboard-tab-overview)); Emma's "Committed Spending" is sourced only from recurring payments and reduces "left to spend" ([Emma help: Committed spending](https://help.emma-app.com/en/article/learn-committed-spending-1cg2t8v/)); Rocket Money shows "left for spending" after bills and goals ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/money/personal-finance/rocket-money-review)); and RiseUp's green weekly number is the same idea with a Sunday–Saturday week ([מה זה שבוע בתזרים?](https://intercom.riseup.co.il/he/articles/8995624)). The third model, envelope ledgers (YNAB, Actual, Lunch Money), has no fixed/flex split at all; YNAB's default groups are Bills, Frequent, Non-Monthly, Goals and Quality of Life Goals ([YNAB blog](https://www.ynab.com/blog/how-many-ynab-categories)), Actual's are Food, Bills, Bills (Flexible), Savings and General ([Actual docs](https://actualbudget.org/docs/getting-started/starting-fresh)).

**None of the sources uses the term "fun money."** The closest objects are Monarch's Flex bucket minus its needs, YNAB's "Quality of Life Goals" (hobbies, health, entertainment), Rocket Money's "Dining And Drinks" plus "Recreation And Entertainment" ([Rocket Money Learn](https://rocketmoney.com/learn/personal-finance/budget-categories)), and the 30% "Wants" of Warren and Tyagi's Balanced Money Formula ([CNN Money, 2005](https://money.cnn.com/magazines/moneymag/moneymag_archive/2005/08/01/8267041/index.htm)); RiseUp's own rule of thumb is 50% fixed, 30% variable plus tracked, 20% savings ([ההוצאות הקבועות תחת זכוכית מגדלת](https://intercom.riseup.co.il/he/articles/8612761)). A discretionary pot is defensible on theory (Fei et al.'s necessity dimension; the bottom-dollar preference for one larger pot over several small ones) but **no experiment tests a fun-money bucket's effect on adherence**; one popular article's "5% allowance" claim could not be verified. finc's existing `lifestyle` group (eating out, leisure, shopping, travel, gifts, Bit to people) is already that pot under a more honest name, and the evidence says give it one group-level target rather than six category targets.

The strongest common denominator across every method is **cadence, not object**: monthly-and-stable → fixed, monthly-but-variable → flexible, less-than-monthly → non-monthly, future-oriented → goals. A car therefore legitimately spans buckets: Monarch puts loan/lease and monthly insurance in Fixed "Cars," fuel and parking in Flexible "Transportation," and maintenance in Non-monthly ([Monarch blog: 23 categories](https://www.monarch.com/the-23-budget-categories-you-need-in-your-budget)); YNAB puts car insurance premiums in Non-Monthly True Expenses ([YNAB blog](https://www.ynab.com/blog/how-many-ynab-categories)); Rocket Money and Lunch Money's starter list force a single "Auto" bucket and trade behavioural clarity for mapping simplicity ([Lunch Money blog](https://lunchmoney.app/blog/how-to-choose-the-right-budget-categories)). finc's `car` group, with `car.insurance` in the fixed section and fuel, maintenance, parking and transit in variable, is exactly the cadence model, and a "car" section in the *list* would re-merge what the taxonomy correctly split.

Installments are where the evidence is thinnest. **No app documentation found specifies where credit-card installment plans ("תשלומים") belong**; US apps treat the card statement as a transfer problem. RiseUp names "הוצאות בתשלומים" among its fixed-expense examples alongside electricity, mortgage and subscriptions, so an installment becomes fixed by recurrence, with no evidence of a "2/6" rendering ([מה לבדוק כשהתזרים שלילי?](https://intercom.riseup.co.il/he/articles/6228720)). Israeli card apps present installments as a product (split your bill) more than a tracking aid; the only tracking affordance found is Isracard's December 2024 "marker for the last installment payment" ([Isracard App Store](https://apps.apple.com/il/app/isracard-ישראכרט/id489783331)). Apple Card's installments "automatically appear on your Apple Card statement alongside your everyday purchases," with detail on a separate Monthly Installments screen ([Apple Support, 2022 archive](https://webcf.waybackmachine.org/web/20220123000208/https://support.apple.com/kb/HT210644)). finc's design, a separate `installments` bucket inside the fixed section with n/total per row and remaining slices projected in upcoming, is more explicit than anything in the field; the evidence neither confirms nor contradicts it.

Person-to-person payments and refunds have clearer consensus. Three cases must be modelled: own-account transfers are excluded from spend everywhere (Copilot, Spendee, Rocket Money, Emma, Lunch Money, Actual); payment *to* a person for goods or a shared bill is an expense ("sending money to a friend for dinner is an expense, not a transfer"); and repayment *from* a person should offset the original spend rather than count as income ([Finny blog](https://getfinny.app/blog/track-venmo-zelle-payments-budget); [Rocket Money help: Non-spending categories](https://help.rocketmoney.com/en/articles/10770105-understanding-non-spending-categories-in-your-budget)). YNAB's rule is the cleanest: if an inflow offsets an expense already recorded, "categorize it back to the original spending category," and credit-card refunds go "to the original spending category, not Ready to Assign" ([YNAB support: Inflows](https://support.ynab.com/en_us/how-to-add-income-and-other-inflows-H1ZNjfZJi.md); [YNAB support: Refunds](https://support.ynab.com/en_us/credit-card-refunds-and-returns-H1J7qDWkj.md)). Copilot says "a refund for a returned purchase should be marked Regular" so it nets against the category ([Copilot help: Transaction types](https://intercom.help/copilotmoney/en/articles/3971267-transaction-types)); Tiller's minority pattern is an offsetting Transfer-type "Reimbursement" category applied to both sides ([Tiller](https://intercom.help/tiller/en/articles/432687-understanding-transfers)). RiseUp's advice for the Bit case is the Tiller pattern by hand: when you pay for a friend, both the outlay and the repayment should be marked "לא בתזרים," or keep your own restaurant share in "אוכל בחוץ" and mark the friend's share and repayment not-in-cashflow ([מה נחשב "לא בתזרים"?](https://intercom.riseup.co.il/he/articles/8995719); [איך להשתמש בתזרים ביומיום?](https://intercom.riseup.co.il/he/articles/8995730)). **Nothing was found on Bit or PayBox detection in any Israeli app**; the Venmo/Zelle patterns are assumed to transfer, and this is where finc's open Bit decision should be marked as a judgement call.

## Row anatomy, filters and Hebrew numbers: what the guidance actually constrains

Platform guidance converges on a row contract that finc's new one-line row already honours. Material 3 requires only a container and label text; supporting text is optional and capped at "one to three lines"; trailing text carries "a price, count, or date"; leading visuals sit at the leading edge; and "Don't vary the position of elements within a list" ([Material 3 Lists](https://m3.material.io/components/lists/guidelines)). Apple says "Keep item text succinct" and, for item-heavy content, "list item titles only, letting people choose an item to reveal its content in a detail view" ([Apple HIG: Lists and tables](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/lists-and-tables)). The apps agree: every mobile row is a two-line name plus secondary line with the amount at the trailing edge, status is a small glyph (Copilot's [I]/[T]/[R] badges and light-blue unreviewed dot; Lunch Money's turquoise text for recurring-linked rows), and notes, tags, receipts, maps, splits and rule creation are universally pushed to the detail ([Copilot help: Transaction types](https://intercom.help/copilotmoney/en/articles/3971267-transaction-types); [Lunch Money support: Transaction types](https://support.lunchmoney.app/finances/transactions/transaction-types.md)). Lunch Money's mobile row is literally "Date, Payee, Category and Amount," with notes-on-row still an open request ([Lunch Money feedback](https://feedback.lunchmoney.app/mobile-app/p/mobile-app-display-notes-in-transactions-list-view)). Monzo's rule for what earns a place on the row is the best practitioner test available: account badges appear only when a customer has more than one account because otherwise they are "visual clutter" ([Monzo blog](https://monzo.com/blog/how-we-unified-our-customers-activity-on-the-new-home-screen)). Abstract category icons would not pass that test on their own: "a text label must be present alongside an icon to clarify its meaning," and only home, print and search are near-universal ([NN/g: Icon Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/)). Density complaints in the wild are concrete: Emma's feed was criticised for "chunky boxes with £0" and a weather widget ([Emma community](https://community.emma-app.com/t/new-feed-design-feedback/3335)), pre-2024 Monarch for a list that was "very spread out" and omitted the account ([Simplifi community](https://community.simplifimoney.com/discussion/comment/31511)), Revolut for promotions pushing the list down ([Revolut community](https://community.revolut.com/t/ui-overhaul/147896?page=8)); the consensus "calm" pick, Copilot, an Apple Design Award finalist in 2024, is praised for one hero number, a pace line instead of tables, and putting "financial data front and center without the noise" ([Copilot FAQ](https://www.copilot.money/faq); [Daring Fireball](https://daringfireball.net/linked/2025/12/28/copilot-money)).

One accessibility constraint bites finc's current row directly. WCAG 2.1 SC 1.4.1 requires that "Color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information," and Apple's App Store criteria single out status that differs only by red versus green ([W3C: Understanding SC 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color.html); [App Store Connect Help](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-app-accessibility/differentiate-without-color-alone-evaluation-criteria)). Copilot added +/− signs to amounts in v1.34.0 for this reason ([Copilot feedback](https://feedback.copilot.money/feature-requests/p/accessibility-for-color-blind)). finc's list renders credits green with `sign="never"`, so a credit and a debit of the same magnitude differ only by hue; a "+" on credits fixes it without adding a glyph to the common case.

For filters, the evidence argues for exactly the compromise finc has reached, with two additions. Baymard found 28% of sites give no overview of applied filters, that mobile users reopened the filter UI just to confirm what was active, and that applied filters should be echoed as a row above the list with a count ([Baymard: Applied Filters](https://baymard.com/blog/how-to-design-applied-filters)); NN/g warns that an icon-only filter entry ("cryptic icon composed of lines and circles") is hard to discover and that a text label is worth the space, and that the result count should stay visible ([NN/g: Mobile Faceted Search](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-faceted-search/)). Material's chip rules, if a chip strip is ever added, are that chips appear in sets, labels stay under 20 characters, all sets on a page share one selection mode, and more than two rows should scroll horizontally ([Material 3 Chips](https://m3.material.io/components/chips/guidelines)). finc's labelled filter button that changes to the active bucket name, plus the scope chip, satisfies the discoverability and visibility findings; what is missing is the filtered total that Copilot and Monarch show.

The RTL findings mostly confirm what finc already does and leave one convention open. Digits never reverse: "Hebrew text uses Western Arabic numerals" and "the digits in a specific number ... always appear in the same order" ([Apple HIG: Right to left](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/right-to-left)); Material lists numbers, untranslated text and charts among things not mirrored ([Material: Bidirectionality](https://m2.material.io/design/usability/bidirectionality.html)). Trailing slots do mirror, so in Hebrew the amount sits at the left edge and leading visuals on the right ([Material 3 Chips](https://m3.material.io/components/chips/guidelines)). The danger is the bidi algorithm: plus and minus are weak class ES and currency symbols weak ET, so without isolation they migrate, which Microsoft calls "troublesome for accounting and financial systems" ([Microsoft Learn: Bidirectional support](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dynamics365/fin-ops-core/dev-itpro/user-interface/bidirectional-support)); SAP's remedy is `textDirection: LTR` on the number with `textAlign: End` ([OpenUI5 RTL guide](https://sdk.openui5.org/1.38.62/docs/guide/7e7cd0aed8bd469cbdd9b46035046dd8.html)), and the W3C recommends an inline element with `dir` over control characters ([W3C: Inline bidi markup](https://www.w3.org/International/articles/inline-bidi-markup/index.en.html)). finc's `.num` class (`tabular-nums; direction: ltr; unicode-bidi: isolate`) with `text-end` alignment and a U+2212 prefix is this exact pattern, and tabular lining figures right-aligned are the typographic standard for comparable columns ([Design better data tables](https://mattstromawn.com/writing/tables); [Shopify.dev: Number](https://shopify.dev/docs/api/app-home/latest/web-components/typography-and-content/number.md)). The Hebrew minus belongs on the left, as CLDR's `minusSign` "‎-" (LRM plus hyphen) encodes and as old OpenOffice and GNOME bugs about "5-" versus "-5" attest ([CLDR he/numbers.json](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/unicode-org/cldr-json/main/cldr-json/cldr-numbers-full/main/he/numbers.json); [GNOME bug 322571](https://bugzilla.gnome.org/show_bug.cgi?id=322571)). **The ₪ position is genuinely unsettled**: CLDR's Hebrew currency pattern is "#,##0.00 ¤" (symbol after), RiseUp and Bank Hapoalim write "15,000 ₪" and "1.75 ₪", One Zero mixes "₪10,000" and "100,000 ₪" on one page, and the Academy of the Hebrew Language is relayed as preferring the symbol to the left of the number with no space ([איך התזרים מחושב?](https://intercom.riseup.co.il/he/articles/8890719); [bankhapoalim FAQ](https://www.bankhapoalim.co.il/he/contact-us/faq); [onezerobank.com](https://www.onezerobank.com/); [hamichlol ₪](https://www.hamichlol.org.il/%E2%82%AA)). No usability study tests either placement.

Finally, the behavioural evidence on the overview's tone is less comfortable than "calm" rhetoric suggests. The only randomised field experiment, **3,138 users over 54,750 user-months**, found that a fear-appeal "Financial Sustainability Index" frame *combined with* a context plot comparing it to past monthly spending cut discretionary spending about 15% (restaurants −14%, clothing −20%, travel −24%) for months, while neither the frame nor the comparison alone did anything ([Levi 2026, JFQA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/personal-financial-information-presentation-and-consumer-spending/FB3B05430DC8FFCEC2707C4161E83086)). Common Cents Lab's Branch study (n≈1,100) found a stacked bar conveyed overspending most strongly and that users misunderstood "left to spend" ([Finsights: Common Cents Lab](https://finsights.guide/case-studies/SUoXS5Ob/learning-from-the-lab-building-better-spending-vi)). The practitioner sources on anxiety (Monzo's "designed friction," Nubank's anxiety framing) are motivational, not evaluated; no study measured anxiety as an outcome of UI colour or tone. The honest reading is that a total without a reference point changes nothing, and that the reference (last month at the same day, or the budget) matters more than whether the number is green.

## Recommendation for finc, in priority order

The table ranks each decision by how much it changes the product and how solid the evidence is. "Strong" means multiple independent sources or a controlled study; "convergent practice" means the leading apps agree but nobody tested it; "thin" means inference or a single source.

Items 1 through 3 are the ones that change what the owner builds next. The list should stay what it just became. The sections the owner wants are the right mental model for the *month*, and finc's `monthSummary` already produces them as buckets, so the work is presentation: a Now screen that reads top to bottom as income, committed, saved, spent-so-far by group, and left, with the variable-remaining figure given a reference it currently lacks. Items 4 through 6 are small changes with strong backing: a group target on `lifestyle`, a "+" on credits, a sum above a filtered list. Items 7 and 8 are where finc is ahead of the evidence; the installment bucket and the three-case Bit rule are defensible designs that no competitor documents, and they should be treated as decisions to revisit with the owner's own data rather than as settled. Item 10 is the only place where the research says "pick one"; whichever ₪ position is chosen, the bidi isolation finc already has is what prevents the sign and symbol from wandering.

## Conclusion

The most useful reframing from this research is that "sections somewhat like RiseUp but better" is two separate ideas that RiseUp happened to fuse: a monthly *accounting* into fixed, tracked, savings, variable and excluded, which every serious app and the behavioural literature endorse at roughly five groups, and a *transaction browser*, which RiseUp barely has and which every other app keeps as a flat timeline with filters. finc already separates them in its engine (§7b buckets versus the transaction list) and should keep them separate on screen. The RiseUp users' own words make the case: the praise is for one screen and one number, the complaints are for every place where the section structure got in the way of a transaction.

What the evidence cannot yet tell the owner is which single number to lead with, whether a weekly or monthly residual paces spending better, and whether a visibly named discretionary pot improves adherence; the one experiment that moved behaviour did so with a comparison to the user's own past, not with a layout. finc has something no competitor in this review has: one user's complete, calibrated history. The right next step after shipping the waterfall is to show the hero number with last month's same-day figure beside it and watch, over a few months, whether the owner's own variable spend changes.
