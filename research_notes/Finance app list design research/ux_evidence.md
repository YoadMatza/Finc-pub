# UX evidence and design guidance for transaction lists and spending overviews on mobile (incl. RTL/Hebrew)

Research date: 2026-11-09. Scope: what usability research (NN/g, Baymard, academic/behavioural studies) and platform/design-system guidance (Apple HIG, Material Design, W3C/Unicode, fintech design write-ups) say about laying out a long list of financial transactions and a monthly spending overview on a phone. Facts and findings only; no product recommendations.

General source-quality note: Apple HIG and Material pages carry no study data; they are normative guidance. NN/g articles are expert guidance usually grounded in qualitative studies (sample sizes rarely given). Baymard figures come from e-commerce benchmarks (not finance apps). Behavioural-finance findings (Levi 2026; Common Cents Lab) are the only controlled experiments found, and they concern spending overviews/feedback, not list layout. No controlled study specifically comparing transaction-list layouts was found.

---

## KQ1. What do NN/g, Baymard, Material, Apple HIG and fintech design systems say about list density, grouping headers, secondary text, and how much a row can carry?

### Takeaway
Platform guidance converges on: one required headline per row, optional supporting text capped at 1–3 lines, a trailing "metadata" slot (explicitly named "price, count, or date" by Material), leading visuals anchored to the leading edge, and every element in the same position in every row. There is no empirical "N items per row" threshold in any source found; the limits are normative (Material: 1–3 lines of supporting text; Apple: "keep item text succinct"). Research-based guidance (NN/g) favours condensed over dispersed layouts and lists over cards for homogeneous, scannable, comparable items.

### Cited Findings

**Material Design 3 — Lists guidelines (m3.material.io, current page, undated)**
- Anatomy: "Container and label text are required. All other elements are optional": supporting text, trailing text, trailing icon, trailing selection control, leading avatar/icon/media. — [Material 3 Lists guidelines](https://m3.material.io/components/lists/guidelines)
- "Keep label text brief." "Limit supporting text to one to three lines"; "Truncate supporting text, depending on screen size." — [Material 3 Lists guidelines](https://m3.material.io/components/lists/guidelines)
- "Trailing text can provide additional meta-information about a list item, such as a price, count, or other details." Illustrated as "Use trailing text for supplemental details, like a price, count, or date." — [Material 3 Lists guidelines](https://m3.material.io/components/lists/guidelines)
- Scannability rules: "Place supporting visuals and primary text in the same position in each list item." "Don't vary the position of elements within a list." "Place supporting visuals, like thumbnails, at the leading edge of a row to improve scannability"; Caution: "Avoid placing visuals in the center of a row because it makes the list difficult to scan." — [Material 3 Lists guidelines](https://m3.material.io/components/lists/guidelines)
- "A leading icon should provide a quick visual cue that relates to the item's label text, helping people scan the list." "A trailing icon is often used to communicate status or indicate an action, like Show more." — [Material 3 Lists guidelines](https://m3.material.io/components/lists/guidelines)
- Importance ordering: "Align content by importance: 1. More distinguishing content 2. Less distinguishing content"; primary action area should take up more space than secondary. — [Material 3 Lists guidelines](https://m3.material.io/components/lists/guidelines)
- Grouping/separators: "Gaps or dividers can separate lists into items and groups. Use gaps for contained lists... Limit dividers to uncontained or complex lists, only when a stronger visual separation is necessary." — [Material 3 Lists guidelines](https://m3.material.io/components/lists/guidelines)
- Compact (phone) breakpoint: "Lists should extend edge-to-edge in compact windows. Selecting a list item should open a page with the details." "Reduce the amount of information shown at compact breakpoints"; on larger screens "lists can show more content, like supporting text and larger imagery." — [Material 3 Lists guidelines](https://m3.material.io/components/lists/guidelines)
- Expand/collapse: "List items containing other list items can expand and collapse in a folder-like manner, to reveal or hide content." — [Material 3 Lists guidelines](https://m3.material.io/components/lists/guidelines)
- Line length: "The ideal line length for text is typically between 40 to 60 characters" (up to 120 on large screens). — [Material 3 Lists guidelines](https://m3.material.io/components/lists/guidelines)
- (Material 2 era, surfaced via search:) Material does not recommend going beyond three lines for most list designs; single-line and two-line items are capped by line count. — [Material 2 Lists (design-lists)](https://m2.material.io/go/design-lists)

**Apple Human Interface Guidelines — Lists and tables (change log: updated June 2023)**
- "Prefer displaying text in a list or table... the row-based format is especially well suited to making text easy to scan and read." — [Apple HIG: Lists and tables](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/lists-and-tables)
- "Keep item text succinct so row content is comfortable to read. Short, succinct text can help minimize truncation and wrapping... If each item consists of a large amount of text... you could list item titles only, letting people choose an item to reveal its content in a detail view." — [Apple HIG: Lists and tables](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/lists-and-tables)
- Truncation: "Sometimes, an ellipsis in the middle of text can make an item easier to distinguish because it preserves both the beginning and the end of the content." — [Apple HIG: Lists and tables](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/lists-and-tables)
- Grouping style: "In iOS and iPadOS... the grouped style uses headers, footers, and additional space to separate groups of data." — [Apple HIG: Lists and tables](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/lists-and-tables)
- Disclosure: "Use an info button only to reveal more information about a row's content... If you need to let people drill into a list or table row's subviews, use a disclosure indicator accessory control." Avoid an alphabetical index in a table whose rows have trailing controls (they collide on the trailing side). — [Apple HIG: Lists and tables](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/lists-and-tables)
- watchOS (small-screen) note: "Short lists are easier for people to scan, but sometimes people expect a long list of items... You can help make a long list more manageable by listing the most relevant items and providing a way for people to view more." — [Apple HIG: Lists and tables](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/lists-and-tables)

**Nielsen Norman Group**
- Cards vs lists (Nov 6, 2016, Page Laubheimer): cards are "less appropriate than lists when people search for information"; lists fix each element's position so users can scan predictably (e.g., for price); consistent rows ease comparison; lists are recommended for homogeneous items; cards take more space so fewer fit on screen, increasing reliance on short-term memory. — [NN/g: Cards: UI-Component Definition](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/cards-component/)
- Content dispersion (Oct 13, 2023, Flaherty/Neusesser/Chitale; 13 qualitative usability tests, 4 prototypes): participants had more difficulty finding information on the dispersed (low-density) page than the condensed version; dispersed layouts increased interaction cost, cognitive load, frustration; users "showed a clear preference for a condensed layout"; dispersed pages could seem to hide information, lowering trust. Recommends keeping critical, related content in the same viewport. Article notes accordions "work very well on mobile devices" because they compress content. — [NN/g: Content Dispersion](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/content-dispersion/)
- Defer secondary content for mobile (Jul 31, 2011, Jakob Nielsen): limit the first screen to the minimum needed, defer secondary details; "The less you can see, the more you have to remember"; qualitative evidence (Groupon vs LivingSocial, Wikipedia collapsed sections, where a user said "I can open the bio and not see all the references"). No numeric comprehension figures in the article. — [NN/g: Defer Secondary Content for Mobile](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/defer-secondary-content-for-mobile/)
- Chunking (Mar 20, 2016, Kate Moran): break content into "small, distinct units"; use headings that contrast with body text, bold keywords, lists; keep related items close (Gestalt proximity); use background colours, rules and white space to separate groups; format numbers (phone/card/dates) in the conventional layout for their type. Cites Miller (1956) 7±2 chunks but warns against treating "seven" as a strict UI limit since menus rely on recognition. — [NN/g: How Chunking Helps Content Processing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/chunking/)
- Data tables (Apr 3, 2022, Page Laubheimer): four user tasks (find records by criteria; compare across cells/rows; view/edit a single record; act on records). "two adjacent data points are easy to compare." Make the first column a human-readable identifier; order columns by importance; freeze headers when table exceeds screen; light borders/zebra striping/hover help keep place; make filters discoverable and show clearly when active. Does not cover number alignment or row density. — [NN/g: Data Tables: Four Major User Tasks](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/data-tables/)
- Icon usability (Jul 27, 2014, Aurora Harley): only a few icons (home, print, search) are near-universal; "a text label must be present alongside an icon to clarify its meaning in that particular context"; labels must be visible (hover does not exist on touch); icons are more noticeable on mobile because fewer competing elements. — [NN/g: Icon Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/)

**Baymard (e-commerce benchmarks; applied to product lists, not finance)**
- Applied-filters overview (Oct 6, 2020, updated May 13, 2026): 28% of benchmarked sites show no overview of applied filters; on mobile, users opened the filter interface just to confirm selections because the list gave "no visual confirmation"; users who overlooked an applied filter misjudged the range of items available. — [Baymard: How to Design Applied Filters](https://baymard.com/blog/how-to-design-applied-filters)

**Fintech design write-ups (official blogs)**
- Monzo, "How we unified our customers' activity on the new home screen" (Jan 10, 2024): feed items are "ordered by time"; account badges shown only when a customer has more than a personal account because otherwise they are "visual clutter"; upcoming payments are collapsed into one row per direction with a count and relative date ("By tomorrow"), company logos and summed amount, expanding on tap; transfers between account and pots are deduplicated into one row (pot name as title, source account as subtitle). Rationale stated: "Clarity matters only where it adds value," aggregation "saves screen space." No user-research metrics on layout; transaction-ordering change tested with staff, then community, then an experiment with "no harm in engagement or feedback." — [Monzo blog](https://monzo.com/blog/how-we-unified-our-customers-activity-on-the-new-home-screen)
- Nubank, "How we created Tabs" (Feb 17, 2023): the single long scrolling home "was no longer scalable"; research found customers organise finances into three moments (day-to-day money in/out and bills; planning goals; discovering) → three tabs, with transactions/account/card and payment shortcuts under one tab. Organising by company structure (per product) was explicitly rejected as putting company needs ahead of users'. Qualitative result: customers perceived the new structure "as cleaner and more organized"; >100 metrics monitored daily; no numbers published. — [Nubank Building blog](https://building.nu.com/how-we-created-tabs/)
- Shopify Polaris (app-home web components) Number component: applies tabular numerals so "every digit the same width"; use for values in columns "such as prices, quantities, or totals"; labels should stay in text components "since tabular numerals make ordinary words look uneven"; app must add currency symbol/decimals/thousands separators per locale and round before rendering. — [Shopify.dev: Number](https://shopify.dev/docs/api/app-home/latest/web-components/typography-and-content/number.md)

### Inferences
- Combining Material's anatomy with Monzo's practice yields a de-facto row template used by major fintechs: leading visual (logo/icon), headline (merchant/payee), supporting text (category, account, note), trailing amount (+ optional secondary trailing text such as status/date). Material explicitly caps supporting text at 1–3 lines; no source quantifies how many separate data fields a row tolerates.
- NN/g's density and cards-vs-lists findings imply a dense, uniform, edge-to-edge list is the appropriate default for reviewing many homogeneous transactions, and that extra per-row elements (badges, chips) should earn their place — mirrored by Monzo hiding account badges for single-account users.
- NN/g's icon findings suggest category icons without visible labels will be ambiguous; merchant logos (recognisable brands) are a different case but no source tests them.

### Gaps
- No empirical study found that measures scanning speed or accuracy as a function of items-per-row in a mobile list; all "row content" guidance is normative.
- Wise Design (wise.design/components/list-item) and Pleo Telescope list-item pages returned 404; Revolut and Cash App publish no design-rationale write-ups on transaction lists that I could find. Intuit and Atlassian density guidance not retrieved.
- Baymard's finance-specific findings (if any) are behind the Premium paywall; only e-commerce filter research is public.

## KQ2. Grouping: chronological (by day) vs categorical sectioning; chunking long lists, sticky headers, collapsible sections

### Takeaway
No study directly compares date-grouped vs category-grouped transaction lists. Indirect evidence: NN/g eye-tracking shows headed sections produce the efficient "layer-cake" scan; a CHI 2001 experiment found category-grouped results beat flat lists; production fintech feeds (Monzo) are time-ordered with aggregation for repetitive items, and Nubank's research found users think in "moments" (day-to-day vs planning), not product lines. Sticky headers trade permanent screen space for reachability (NN/g 2021); collapsible sections/accordions are endorsed for mobile by NN/g and Material.

### Cited Findings
- Layer-cake scanning (NN/g, Aug 4, 2019, Kara Pernice, eye-tracking): fixations fall mostly on headings/subheadings with occasional stops on body text; called "by far the most effective way to scan pages" apart from reading everything; pages without clear headings are scanned in the less efficient F-pattern and users miss information. Effective subheadings are visually distinct, clearly associated with their content via spacing, accurately scoped, front-loaded with keywords, brief. — [NN/g: Layer-Cake Pattern of Scanning](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/)
- Category grouping vs list (Dumais, Cutrell & Chen, CHI 2001): seven interfaces tested; "all Category interfaces were more effective than List interfaces," including lists augmented with category labels; best was category names plus page titles. (Domain: web search results, not finance; quantitative figures are in the PDF, which I could not parse.) — [Microsoft Research: Optimizing Search by Showing Results in Context](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/optimizing-search-by-showing-results-in-context/)
- Chunking (NN/g 2016): "Group related items under clear visual hierarchies"; separate groups with white space, rules, background colour; Miller's 7±2 is not a strict UI limit. — [NN/g: Chunking](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/chunking/)
- Sticky headers (NN/g, Apr 4, 2021, Page Laubheimer): benefits = reachability/discoverability of header items without scrolling back; costs = header "permanently uses screen space on every page," distraction, translucency reduces contrast; "If users rarely need the header's items, the space cost outweighs the benefit." Partially persistent headers (reappear on scroll-up) are most common on mobile; slide-in ~300–400 ms. Content-to-chrome ratio examples: 13:1 (The New Yorker, acceptable) vs 2:1 (Lollar Pickups, poor) on iPhone 11 Pro. — [NN/g: Sticky Headers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/sticky-headers/)
- Accordions on mobile: NN/g 2023 says accordions "work very well on mobile devices" because they compress content. — [NN/g: Content Dispersion](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/content-dispersion/); Material 3: nested list items "can expand and collapse in a folder-like manner." — [Material 3 Lists](https://m3.material.io/components/lists/guidelines)
- Progressive disclosure (NN/g, Dec 3, 2006, Jakob Nielsen): "designs that go beyond 2 disclosure levels typically have low usability"; "the very fact that something appears on the initial display tells users that it's important"; decide the split using frequency-of-use data plus observation, not analytics alone. — [NN/g: Progressive Disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- Production practice, time ordering: Monzo's unified feed is "ordered by time" with items from multiple sub-feeds merged; repetitive upcoming payments are aggregated into one expandable row; estimated transaction times keep items from different banks in the order payments were made. — [Monzo blog, Jan 2024](https://monzo.com/blog/how-we-unified-our-customers-activity-on-the-new-home-screen)
- Monzo community (user forum, not official): a user requested grouping transactions into expandable single feed items (bar-tab example); pushback that "a feed should be an accurate timeline" and wrong grouping "is adding a layer of complexity." — [Monzo Community: Group transactions](https://community.monzo.com/t/group-transactions/124588)
- Nubank: research found customers organise finances into three mental "moments" (day-to-day in/out & bills; planning; discovering), used as the top-level grouping. — [Nubank: How we created Tabs](https://building.nu.com/how-we-created-tabs/)
- Material 3: "Use gaps for contained lists... Limit dividers to uncontained or complex lists, only when a stronger visual separation is necessary." — [Material 3 Lists](https://m3.material.io/components/lists/guidelines)
- Apple HIG: grouped style "uses headers, footers, and additional space to separate groups of data." — [Apple HIG: Lists and tables](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/lists-and-tables)
- Informal/secondary design critiques (low evidence weight): a Pratt design critique of the Discover Bank iOS app recommended placing the date at the top and grouping same-day transactions "to increase proximity." — [Pratt IXD critique](https://ixd.prattsi.org/2024/09/design-critique-discover-bank-ios-mobile-app/); a student GPay case study noted history "grouped by month... easy for the user to understand which month this transaction belongs to." — [Medium: GPay transaction history](https://medium.com/@poorani671994/an-attempt-to-optimize-the-search-functionality-of-gpay-transaction-history-in-order-to-boost-the-86edde0c7303)

### Inferences
- Evidence supports visible section headers of some kind (layer-cake scanning; CHI 2001 category advantage) over a flat undifferentiated list; it does not adjudicate date vs category. Date headers suit the "accurate timeline" reconciliation task (Monzo community sentiment; Monzo's own time-ordering), while category grouping suits review/overview tasks (CHI 2001 analogue; Nubank "moments").
- Sticky date headers cost screen space per NN/g; the NN/g cost-benefit test ("do users need the header's items while scrolling?") suggests sticky headers are justified only if knowing the current day/month while scrolling is a frequent need.
- NN/g's two-level progressive-disclosure limit implies list → detail sheet is the maximum depth before usability degrades; a third level (e.g., group → sub-group → detail) should be avoided or flattened.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found comparing day-grouped vs category-grouped financial transaction lists on mobile, nor measuring sticky-section-header benefit in long lists. Searches on HCI venues via web returned only patents and student case studies.
- Chen/Dumais quantitative figures (time, preference) not extracted (PDF unparsable in this environment).

## KQ3. Progressive disclosure in finance apps: row vs detail sheet; merchant logos/icons; colour for amounts and accessibility

### Takeaway
Platform guidance says phones should show a compact row and open a full detail page on tap (Material compact breakpoint), with at most two disclosure levels (NN/g). Icons need visible labels to be understood (NN/g); Material frames leading icons as scanning aids. WCAG 1.4.1 forbids colour as the only carrier of meaning, so red/green amounts need a sign, arrow or label; several finance apps (ZipBooks, Futubull, Copilot, YNAB) have retrofitted non-colour cues.

### Cited Findings
- Material 3 compact breakpoint: "Selecting a list item should open a page with the details"; "Reduce the amount of information shown at compact breakpoints"; on larger screens show list and detail side-by-side. — [Material 3 Lists](https://m3.material.io/components/lists/guidelines)
- Apple HIG: list titles only, with details revealed in a detail view, when items carry a lot of text; disclosure indicator (chevron) for drill-down, info button only for "more information about a row's content." — [Apple HIG: Lists and tables](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/lists-and-tables)
- NN/g progressive disclosure: two levels max; initial display signals importance; make the progression control visible and labelled (information scent). — [NN/g: Progressive Disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- NN/g data tables: nonmodal side panels let users edit while seeing the table; modal popups hide adjacent records users need for reference; accordions inside tables clutter and hinder referencing nearby records. — [NN/g: Data Tables](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/data-tables/)
- Icons: "Obscure icon = wasted feature"; only home/print/search near-universal; visible text label required; icons more noticeable on mobile. — [NN/g: Icon Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/)
- Material: leading icon "should provide a quick visual cue that relates to the item's label text, helping people scan the list"; use circular avatars for a person or entity, square/rectangular images for other content; anchor visuals to the leading edge. — [Material 3 Lists](https://m3.material.io/components/lists/guidelines)
- Monzo: account badges only when the user has multiple accounts (otherwise "visual clutter"); aggregated upcoming-payments row shows company logos and the summed amount; tapping lists the individual payments. — [Monzo blog, Jan 2024](https://monzo.com/blog/how-we-unified-our-customers-activity-on-the-new-home-screen)
- WCAG 2.1 SC 1.4.1 (Level A): "Color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information"; where meaning depends on distinguishing specific colours (e.g., green valid / red invalid outline example), an additional visual indicator is required regardless of contrast; a lightness difference counts only with ≥3:1 contrast. Sufficient techniques G14 (information in text) and G111 (colour + pattern). — [W3C Understanding SC 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color.html)
- Apple App Store accessibility criteria: a status indicator that differs only by red vs green "may not be easy for people with red-green color-blindness to perceive." — [App Store Connect Help: Differentiate without color alone](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-app-accessibility/differentiate-without-color-alone-evaluation-criteria)
- Industry practice: ZipBooks added a "Color Accessibility Mode" swapping red/green for blue/orange on transactions — [ZipBooks blog](https://zipbooks.com/blog/color-accessibility-mode/); Futubull revised its "Red Up Green Down" scheme for colour-blind users — [Android Developers story](https://developer.android.com/stories/apps/futu); Copilot Money added +/− signs to transaction amounts (v1.34.0) while a colour-blind-mode request remained open — [Copilot feedback](https://feedback.copilot.money/feature-requests/p/accessibility-for-color-blind); YNAB added visual signals to "Available" amounts but still uses red/green — [YNAB](https://www.ynab.com/a-budget-thats-easy-on-the-eyes).
- Prevalence (secondary, agency blog; treat cautiously): red-green colour deficiency ≈8% of men, 0.5% of women; a fintech designer found red negative / green positive looked "almost identical" under deuteranopia. — [Glance: designing for colour blindness](https://thisisglance.com/learning-centre/how-do-i-design-for-users-with-colour-blindness)

### Inferences
- From WCAG 1.4.1 and Apple's criteria: if income is green and expenses neutral/red, the sign (+/−), direction glyph or wording must also carry the distinction; colour can only be redundant. A scheme where expenses are neutral (no colour) and only credits are coloured still relies on colour to flag credits unless a "+" is present.
- NN/g's two-level rule plus Material's compact-breakpoint rule frame the mobile pattern as: row (level 0) → detail (level 1); any further nesting (e.g., detail → sub-detail) is the usability-risky third level.

### Gaps
- No study found on whether merchant logos improve recognition/scan speed in transaction lists; only normative guidance about leading visuals and (for abstract icons) the need for labels.
- No finance-specific research found on which fields belong on the row vs detail (e.g., whether users need category or account on the row). Monzo's badge rule is the only documented practitioner decision.

## KQ4. Filters and search on mobile: chips row vs filter sheet vs segmented control; discoverability; overuse of chips

### Takeaway
Material positions filter chips as an alternative to segmented buttons/checkboxes for filtering a list, mandates sets (never a lone chip), ≤20-character labels, horizontal scrolling beyond two rows, and a single selection mode per page; Baymard and NN/g research shows mobile filter overlays hide applied state, so applied filters must be echoed visibly (horizontal row/count label) and the filter entry point should be a labelled text button, not an icon.

### Cited Findings
- Material 3 Chips: "Filter chips... can be a good alternative to segmented buttons or checkboxes when viewing a list or search results." Single-select chip sets offer "an alternative to segmented buttons, radio buttons, or single select menus." "avoid mixing chip set behaviors. All chip sets on a page should be either single-select or multi-select." — [Material 3 Chips guidelines](https://m3.material.io/components/chips/guidelines)
- "Chip label text should be 20 characters or fewer"; "Don't display a single chip by itself. Chips should appear in a set." "Filter chips should not present only a single option." "Filter chips can wrap to a new row. If there are more than two rows, consider using horizontal scrolling." "Use a side sheet to organize many filter chips." Write filter chips as nouns; avoid negatives like "Exclude images." 48dp minimum target; 8dp minimum gap. — [Material 3 Chips guidelines](https://m3.material.io/components/chips/guidelines)
- "Chips aren't buttons": chips are "dynamic to the situation" and "represent forking paths for a current task, while buttons represent linear steps"; "Avoid replacing major actions with chips." In compact windows a chip's trailing remove icon "is too small to be accessible on its own." — [Material 3 Chips guidelines](https://m3.material.io/components/chips/guidelines)
- Baymard applied filters (2020, upd. 2026): on mobile, show applied filters as a horizontally scrolling row above the list, truncating the right-most item (show at least half of it) and/or fading the edge; add a count label ("4 Filters Applied") but not alone; stacked rows push content down, so show 1–2 rows with "See All"; mobile users had to reopen the filter UI for each removal, which discouraged fine-tuning. 28% of sites show no applied-filter overview. — [Baymard: How to Design Applied Filters](https://baymard.com/blog/how-to-design-applied-filters)
- Baymard filter UI: filters "should occupy their own layer — either a full-screen overlay or a bottom sheet — triggered by a clearly labeled button"; mobile filters are usually "not permanently visible as they are on many desktop sites." — [Baymard: Ecommerce filter UI](https://baymard.com/learn/ecommerce-filter-ui)
- NN/g mobile faceted search (Jul 26, 2015, Kathryn Whitenton): push-out tray over results gives "continuous visibility of results"; full-screen facet screens separate controls from results (criticised); icon-only filter entry ("cryptic icon composed of lines and circles") is hard to discover — text labels "Filter"/"Refine" are worth the space; keep the result count visible and updating promptly. Article predates chips and does not discuss them. — [NN/g: Mobile Faceted Search with a Tray](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-faceted-search/)
- NN/g data tables (2022): "Make filters discoverable and show clearly when they are active." — [NN/g: Data Tables](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/data-tables/)
- Segmented controls (design-system guidance, not research): Toyota Europe design system says segmented controls suit 2–3 options (occasionally up to 5), single choice; use chips/checkboxes for multi-select; distinguish from tabs (navigation) and toggles (binary). — [Toyota DX playbook: Segmented control](https://dx-playbook-prev.toyota-europe.com/cds/components/forms/segmented-control-pages/design/)
- Bright HR design system: chips "offer the benefit of being able to see which values you can filter by"; chip labels must be clear because chips "may not have a specific category." — [Bright UI docs: Filtering](https://ui-docs.bright.hr/docs/design-patterns/Filtering)

### Inferences
- Material's rules imply a visible chip row is appropriate for a small number of high-frequency filters (fits in ≤2 rows or a scrolling strip), while larger facet sets belong in a sheet with chips echoing the applied state — the same two-layer pattern Baymard recommends.
- NN/g's icon findings + Baymard's filter-visibility findings together argue against an icon-only filter button and against hiding active filters.

### Gaps
- No study found quantifying "chip overuse" (e.g., decline in use beyond N chips) or comparing chips row vs bottom sheet vs segmented control for filtering a transaction list; all comparisons are normative.
- Baymard's full mobile filtering benchmark is paywalled.

## KQ5. Number formatting and scannability; RTL (Hebrew) specifics: alignment, mixed-direction text, amount column position, icon mirroring

### Takeaway
Typographic and design-system guidance: numbers in columns use tabular lining figures, right-aligned (LTR), consistent decimals, minimal significant figures. For Hebrew: digits never reverse (Apple, Material, W3C); the Unicode bidi algorithm treats minus (ES) and currency symbols (ET) as weak, so signs/symbols can jump sides unless the amount is isolated or marked with LRM/RLM; CLDR's Hebrew currency pattern is "#,##0.00 ¤" (symbol after the number, with RLM marks) and the minus sign carries an LRM. Trailing slots mirror in RTL (Material: trailing icon moves to the left), so an "amount at trailing edge" row places amounts on the left in Hebrew.

### Cited Findings

**Number typography and alignment**
- Matthew Ström, "Design better data tables" (undated): "Numerical data is right-aligned" because readers compare from the ones digit; text left-aligned; headers share column alignment; avoid centre alignment (ragged, harder to scan); use tabular, lining figures so digits line up (Work Sans free; San Francisco has them; enable via CSS font-feature-settings); keep the same number of decimal digits per column; "use as few significant figures as you can"; well-aligned data makes rules largely unnecessary; horizontal rules should be very light. — [Design better data tables](https://mattstromawn.com/writing/tables)
- Fonts.com Fontology: proportional figures for numerals in running text; tabular figures preferable in columns because every digit has the same width. — [Fonts.com: Proportional vs Tabular Figures](https://www.fonts.com/content/learning/fontology/level-3/numbers/proportional-vs-tabular-figures)
- Shopify Polaris Number component: tabular numerals so digits "stack in columns without shifting width"; add currency symbol, decimals and separators per locale; round before rendering. — [Shopify.dev: Number](https://shopify.dev/docs/api/app-home/latest/web-components/typography-and-content/number.md)
- NN/g chunking: format number strings (card numbers, phone, dates) in the conventional grouping for that type to reduce slips. — [NN/g: Chunking](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/chunking/)
- Maersk design system built a wrapper to prevent Intl.NumberFormat from rounding currencies (practitioner signal that rounding of money values is treated as a defect). — [Maersk Design System: Numbers localisation](https://designsystem.maersk.com/content/numbers/localisation/index.html)

**Digits and direction in RTL**
- Apple HIG, Right to left: "Don't reverse the order of numerals in a specific number. Regardless of the current language or the surrounding content, the digits in a specific number — such as '541,' a phone number, or a credit card number — always appear in the same order." "Hebrew text uses Western Arabic numerals." "Reverse the order of numerals that show progress or a counting direction; never flip the numerals themselves." — [Apple HIG: Right to left](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/right-to-left)
- Apple HIG alignment: "Use a consistent alignment for all text items in a list... reverse the alignment of all items in a list, including items that are displayed in a different script." Align 1–2-line text to the context direction but align a paragraph (3+ lines) to its own language. Hebrew/Arabic next to uppercase Latin may need ~2pt larger size to balance. — [Apple HIG: Right to left](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/right-to-left)
- Material Design Bidirectionality (M2, undated): not mirrored: "Numbers", "Untranslated text (even if it's part of a phrase)", "Icons that don't communicate direction, such as a camera", "Numbers, such as those on a clock and phone numbers", "Charts and graphs". Mirrored: text-field icons swap sides, navigation buttons reverse, directional arrows flip, translated text right-aligns; "Progress bars fill in the same direction as content is read"; "Placement of units may vary across languages." Example: "An Arabic UI in RTL, with numbers presented in LTR." — [Material: Bidirectionality](https://m2.material.io/design/usability/bidirectionality.html)
- Material 3 Chips: trailing icon "On the right for left-to-right (LTR) languages... On the left for right-to-left (RTL) languages" — i.e., trailing slots mirror. — [Material 3 Chips](https://m3.material.io/components/chips/guidelines)
- W3C i18n, UBA basics: "Numeric digits run left-to-right, but don't break directional runs"; "certain otherwise neutral characters, such as currency symbols, will be treated as part of the number rather than a neutral"; a neutral between strong characters of different directions takes the base direction (hence a trailing comma/exclamation mark after RTL text in an LTR base direction lands on the wrong side); isolate opposite-direction phrases with an inline element carrying `dir` rather than control characters. — [W3C: Unicode Bidirectional Algorithm basics](https://www.w3.org/International/articles/inline-bidi-markup/uba-basics); [W3C: Inline markup and bidirectional text in HTML](https://www.w3.org/International/articles/inline-bidi-markup/index.en.html)
- Unicode bidi classes (as tabulated in Microsoft's Dynamics 365 bidi doc, updated Dec 31, 2024): plus and minus signs are weak type ES (European Number Separator); currency symbols, degree sign are weak ET (European Number Terminator); comma/period/colon are CS; digits EN. Weak characters take direction from neighbouring strong text, so "the algorithm will move characters around within the same string... This issue is troublesome for accounting and financial systems." Worked examples: "Dynamics (DAT)" renders as "(Dynamics(DAT" in RTL context; "(701)225-2188" renders ")701(225-2188". Recommended remedy: flag numeric/identifier fields as LTR for input while right-aligning them for display; phone/SSN/document numbers "are always read LTR." — [Microsoft Learn: Right-to-left language support and bidirectional text](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dynamics365/fin-ops-core/dev-itpro/user-interface/bidirectional-support)
- SAP OpenUI5 RTL guidance: numerals and LTR content such as phone numbers, dates and currency values can appear flipped in RTL; set `textDirection: LTR` on the number-bearing control and `textAlign: End` (not a fixed Left/Right) so the number stays LTR internally while aligned to the page's end edge; controls that enforce alignment need only the direction property; "Test in RTL mode with real data." — [OpenUI5: API Properties for RTL Support](https://sdk.openui5.org/1.38.62/docs/guide/7e7cd0aed8bd469cbdd9b46035046dd8.html)
- CLDR Hebrew (he) locale data (cldr-json main): defaultNumberingSystem "latn"; decimal "."; group ","; minusSign "‎-" (LEFT-TO-RIGHT MARK + hyphen-minus); decimal pattern "#,##0.###"; percent "#,##0%"; currency standard and accounting patterns both "‏#,##0.00 ‏¤;‏-#,##0.00 ‏¤" (RIGHT-TO-LEFT MARKs, number first, space, currency symbol after; negative = leading minus); currencySpacing inserts a space between digit and symbol. — [CLDR he/numbers.json](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/unicode-org/cldr-json/main/cldr-json/cldr-numbers-full/main/he/numbers.json)
- Conflicting practice: the open-source Hebrew finance app "Spent" documents that in Hebrew mode currency "still leads with ₪" (symbol before the number) while commas/grouping follow Hebrew conventions; list direction is RTL but "each merchant name renders in its own native direction"; bank logos are not flipped; charts only partly mirror (y-axis stays left). — [Spent: Hebrew & RTL](https://shaya16.github.io/Spent/hebrew-and-rtl/)
- Historical Hebrew minus-sign convention (OpenOffice bug thread, c. 2011; low authority): Hebrew popular usage places the minus to the left of the number; proposed fix was inserting an LRM before the minus — consistent with CLDR's current minusSign "‎-" carrying an LRM. — [Apache OpenOffice Bugzilla 100737](https://bz-he-de.apache.org/ooo/show_bug.cgi?id=100737)
- Zen Cart Hebrew storefront bug: a negative price's sign rendered as a dash on the wrong side of the currency symbol ("resembles a dash and not a negative") — illustration of the ES/ET weak-class problem. — [Zen Cart forum](https://www.zen-cart.com/showthread.php/182597-Attribute-drop-down-box-�-RTL-and-plus-minus-signs-alignment�)

**Icon mirroring (RTL)**
- Apple: flip controls showing progress/navigation (back points right in RTL); "Preserve the direction of a control that refers to an actual direction"; flip icons representing text/reading direction or forward/backward motion; "Don't flip logos or universal signs and marks" (checkmark); "avoid flipping interface icons that depict real-world objects" (clock, pencil, game controller); consider component-level decisions for complex icons (badge position, slash kept as backslash in both directions). — [Apple HIG: Right to left](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/right-to-left)
- Material: mirror back/forward and anything depicting linear time or forward motion; do not mirror clocks, circular refresh/history arrows, physical objects (keyboard, headset), right-handed objects (search magnifier, café cup), slashes, media playback controls and media progress; charts and graphs not mirrored. — [Material: Bidirectionality](https://m2.material.io/design/usability/bidirectionality.html)

### Inferences
- In a Hebrew transaction row built on the standard anatomy, "trailing" = left edge: amounts sit on the left, leading logos on the right. Material's and Apple's "right-align numbers" (LTR) guidance translates to end-aligning the amount; OpenUI5's `textAlign: End` + `textDirection: LTR` encodes exactly this: amount internally LTR, aligned to the layout's end edge.
- CLDR's Hebrew currency pattern (symbol after the number, "12.50 ₪") conflicts with at least one Israeli app's choice ("₪12.50"); no usability evidence found for either. Whichever is chosen, the amount string must be bidi-isolated (dir="ltr" or `<bdi>`, or CLDR's embedded LRM/RLM) so the minus and ₪ do not migrate.
- Material's "charts and graphs are not mirrored" vs Apple's "flip controls that show progress" leaves a spending bar/progress indicator ambiguous: a time-axis chart stays LTR under Material; a "spent so far" progress bar flips under both (Material: "Progress bars fill in the same direction as content is read").

### Gaps
- No usability study found on amount-column position (left vs right) or on currency-symbol placement for Hebrew readers; only standards (CLDR) and one app's documentation.
- No study found on decimals vs rounded amounts in transaction lists; only practitioner guidance (Ström: minimal significant figures; Maersk: avoid rounding currencies) which pulls in opposite directions for different contexts (overview vs ledger).
- Google Fonts Knowledge article on tabular type could not be fetched (JS-rendered).

## KQ6. "Calm" / low-anxiety finance UI: alarm colours, when to show totals, framing of overspending — behavioural evidence

### Takeaway
The controlled evidence is mixed and does not simply favour "calm": a 2026 randomised field experiment (n=3,138) found a fear-appeal "sustainability" frame combined with a salient comparison to past spending cut discretionary spending ~15% for months, while neither frame nor salience alone did anything; Common Cents Lab's Branch study (n≈1,100, hypothetical choices) found "overdraft risk" framing shifted intent to cut spending more than "looks great" framing, and that a stacked bar conveyed overspending most strongly but users misunderstood "left to spend". Budgeting features alone showed no financial effect (Clarity Money). Practitioner and policy sources (Monzo, Nubank, Money & Mental Health) argue for designed friction, opt-in alerts and clarity, framed around anxiety but without outcome data on UI colour or tone.

### Cited Findings
- Levi, Y. (2026), "Personal Financial Information Presentation and Consumer Spending," Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis 61(5): 2455–2480 (online Mar 24, 2026). Randomised field experiment in a free account-aggregation app, 3,138 users / 54,750 user-months. Treatments: index labelled "Financial Sustainability Index" (consumption frame, fear-appeal wording) vs "Life Annuity Index" (neutral); with vs without a "context plot" comparing the index to historical monthly spending. Only consumption-frame + context-plot groups reduced spending: discretionary spending −≈15% (≈$553/month), restaurants −14%, clothing −20%, entertainment −14%, travel −24%, cash withdrawals −25%; total spending −6% (10% significance); groceries/gas/utilities unchanged. Effect lasted the 8 months shown, persisted ~4 months after removal (10% level), converged by ~month 16. Logins rose ~1.3/month in all treated groups. Abstract: "minor variations in information presentation can significantly affect financial behavior." — [Cambridge Core: JFQA article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/personal-financial-information-presentation-and-consumer-spending/FB3B05430DC8FFCEC2707C4161E83086)
- Common Cents Lab (Duke) × Branch, "Building better spending visuals" (data from 2020; Qualtrics survey via push notification; n=1,145 behavioural, 1,082 interpretation, 1,043 preference). Three visuals (progress bar, gas gauge, stacked bar) × two framings ("looks great" vs "overdraft risk"). Gas gauge and stacked bar: "looks great" users more likely to keep spending, "overdraft risk" users more likely to cut (stacked bar p=0.0003; gauge p=0.0007; progress bar n.s. p=0.16). Hypothetical food choice: "overdraft risk" increased choosing to cook over delivery across all visuals (p=0.007/0.004/<0.0001). Lessons: users needed clearer explanation of "left to spend"; stacked bar gave the strongest impression of overspending (shows fraction of paycheck spent); understanding rate of spend required "higher data visualization literacy than this user base had." Caveats: no clear no-visual control; hypothetical decisions. — [Finsights Guide (Common Cents Lab case study)](https://finsights.guide/case-studies/SUoXS5Ob/learning-from-the-lab-building-better-spending-vi)
- Common Cents Lab × Clarity Money budgeting field experiment: budgeting feature increased app engagement but "neither positive nor negative financial impacts from budgeting." — [Finsights Guide: Clarity Money budgeting experiment](https://case-studies.advanced-hindsight.com/case-studies/GfCI1P8I/clarity-money-budgeting-experiment); Irrational Labs: "no evidence that budgets helped people spend less"; feedback should "guide without overwhelming or demoralizing users." — [Irrational Labs](https://irrationallabs.com/?p=5545)
- Huang & Liang (SJDM 2018 poster, as summarised in search results; PDF unreachable): receiving spending feedback vs none "leads to an increase in spending over time, when there is money slack in the budget"; framing feedback as "discrete success checks attenuates spending acceleration." Unverified poster-level evidence. — [UBC dlab SJDM poster](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/presentations/2018-Poster-Huang-Liang-Budget-Feedback-Spending~.pdf)
- CFPB, "Consumer Insights on Managing Spending" (Feb 28, 2017): tested a payment card giving real-time spending feedback at point of sale plus an app showing budget remaining; "over 90 percent of the consumers we talked to were interested"; perceived benefits: curb impulse spending, easier budgeting, reduced uncertainty; budgeting/tracking often seen as "overwhelming or too much of a hassle." Results described as not statistically representative. — [CFPB report page](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/consumer-insights-managing-spending/)
- Monzo, "Designing a product with mental health issues in mind" (Jan 27, 2017, Zander Brade): proposes "considered, designed friction" (blister-pack analogy), next-day "do you still want this?" prompts for online purchases, optional trusted-person alerts for purchases over £100, non-accusatory prompts when salary stops, user-chosen support channel/time to reduce anxiety. Cites Money & Mental Health finding that people with bipolar disorder overspent late at night in manic phases; Rethink: 1 in 4 adults with a mental-health problem is in problem debt. Does not address how spending is displayed; ideas marked speculative. — [Monzo blog](https://monzo.com/blog/2017/01/27/designing-product-mental-health-mind/)
- Monzo, "A better way to see how your money's trending" (Aug 16, 2022): Trends shows balance after upcoming bills/direct debits ("£600 balance with £150 council tax due shows £450 left to spend"), a tappable balance graph by day, swipe back through months; rationale: people overspend on payday weekend and feel short later, so seeing what's left should help them "pace spending and feel more in control." No formal research cited; does not describe how overspending is displayed. — [Monzo blog](https://monzo.com/blog/balance-in-trends)
- Nubank (2023): cites WHO data that Brazil is "the most anxious country in the world" (~9% with an anxiety disorder) as context for simplifying the app; outcome reported qualitatively ("cleaner and more organized"). — [Nubank Building](https://building.nu.com/how-we-created-tabs/)
- Money and Mental Health Policy Institute (UK): people wanted opt-in tools such as weekly/monthly spending limits as "an effective block to impulsive spending"; banks can use their spending overview to spot problems and alert on sudden increases; solutions should protect "but should not undermine their autonomy." — [MMHPI: online shopping, banks, retailers](https://www.moneyandmentalhealth.org/online-shopping-banks-retailers/); [MMHPI: financial inclusion](https://www.moneyandmentalhealth.org/making-financial-inclusion-deliver-for-people-with-mental-health-problems/)
- Accessibility angle on alarm colours: WCAG 1.4.1 requires a non-colour cue alongside red/green (see KQ3). — [W3C SC 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color.html)

### Inferences
- The two experiments agree that framing changes behaviour only when paired with a salient comparison (Levi: index vs past spending; CCL: visual fraction of paycheck spent). A spending overview that shows a total without reference (budget, prior month, paycheck) is unlikely to change behaviour either way, by this evidence.
- "Calm" (non-alarming) presentation is supported by practitioner/policy sources on anxiety and by the CCL lesson that users were confused by "left to spend", but the controlled evidence shows alarm framing ("overdraft risk", "sustainability" fear appeal) is what moved spending. The literature therefore frames this as a trade-off (behaviour change vs comfort/trust), not a settled recommendation; none of the studies measured anxiety as an outcome.
- Huang & Liang's (unverified) result suggests continuous feedback can license spending when there is slack — relevant to always-visible "left to spend" totals.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures anxiety, stress or trust as an outcome of finance-UI colour, tone or total placement; the anxiety framing in Monzo/Nubank/MMHPI sources is motivational, not evaluated.

- CFPB full PDF could not be text-extracted in this environment; findings are from the report's landing page.
- Full Levi paper beyond the first ~100k characters (robustness/heterogeneity sections) not read.
